Scope discipline: the boring superpower

Scope is the least exciting part of security research and the one that matters most. Every technique I have written about — recon, bundle tracing, manual probing — sits inside a boundary someone agreed to. The moment a request falls outside that boundary, the technique does not matter anymore. Discipline about scope is what separates research from trespassing, and it is mostly a paperwork habit, not a technical one.

Scope first. Everything on this blog assumes written authorization for the target. No scope, no recon — that rule is non-negotiable in my workflow, and this post is about how I actually enforce it on myself.

1. Written authorization first

Authorization must be written and specific: named assets, what is allowed, what is excluded, and a contact on the other side. A vague "take a look at our stuff" from a friend is not authorization. When I work through a bug bounty program, the program's policy page is the written scope — I save a dated copy of it the day the engagement starts, because policies get edited and memory is unreliable:

$ mkdir -p engagement-2026-10-07 && cd engagement-2026-10-07
$ curl -s https://example-program.com/policy > policy-2026-10-07.html
$ echo "2026-10-07: policy snapshot saved; scope reviewed below" > notes.md

If there is no program page and no signed document, there is no engagement. I have walked away from interesting-looking targets on exactly this basis.

2. Write the boundary down

Every engagement folder I create gets a scope.md file before anything else. It lists what is in scope, what is explicitly out, and any special conditions (rate limits, no automated scanning, no social engineering). It takes five minutes and removes an entire class of mistakes:

$ cat scope.md
# Scope — engagement 2026-10-07
#
# IN SCOPE:
#   app.example.com, api.example.com
#
# OUT OF SCOPE (do not touch):
#   *.example.com (all other hosts)
#   blog.example.com, status.example.com
#
# CONDITIONS: manual testing only, business hours, report to X

This file is the single source of truth for the engagement. When a subdomain list or a JS bundle hints at a new host, the first question is not "what is it" — it is "is it on the list".

3. Re-read the boundary before every session

Scope drift is real. You resolve 400 subdomains, one of them is interesting, and you forget that only three of them were authorized. My counter is a thirty-second ritual at the start of each session: open scope.md, read it, and filter every target list through it before sending a single request:

# keep only hosts that are explicitly in scope
$ printf 'app.example.com\napi.example.com\n' > in-scope.txt
$ grep -Fxf in-scope.txt targets-today.txt > targets-checked.txt
$ diff <(sort targets-today.txt) <(sort targets-checked.txt)
# anything listed by diff is dropped — no exceptions

It feels pedantic. That is the point. The discipline has to be mechanical, because judgment gets tired at 2 a.m. and checklists do not.

4. Learn the gray zones

Real scopes have edges that are not obvious. A few that come up constantly:

  • Subsidiaries and white-labels. Same product, different company name, possibly a different legal entity — often out of scope unless named.
  • Third-party services the target uses. Payment processors, CDNs, analytics endpoints — they belong to someone else.
  • Staging and dev environments found during recon. Interesting does not mean authorized.
  • Other users' data you stumble into while testing your own account. Seeing it is an accident; going back for more is a decision.

My rule for all of these: if I have to argue with myself about whether it counts, it does not count. I note it in the dead-end log and move on — or ask the program/contact first.

5. When something interesting is out of scope

It will happen: during authorized recon you notice a misconfiguration on a host that is explicitly excluded. The correct response is boring: stop, take a dated note, and do not touch it again. If the host belongs to someone who can act on it, a short, factual message to their security contact is appropriate. What you do not do is "just take a quick look to confirm" — confirmation is testing, and testing is what the scope governs.

Why this compounds

Scope discipline pays for itself in three ways. It keeps your accounts and your name in good standing with programs. It makes your reports credible — vendors listen differently to a researcher whose methodology is visibly clean. And it removes the background anxiety from the work: every request I send, I can point to the line in scope.md that allows it. That is the boring superpower. Techniques find issues; discipline lets you keep finding them.

Disclosure note. Everything here is methodology only. Specific findings from live engagements are reported privately to the vendor first and published here only after remediation or explicit permission.
