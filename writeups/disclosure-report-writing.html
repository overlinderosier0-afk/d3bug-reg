How to write a disclosure report

Finding a bug is half the work. The other half is getting a vendor to understand it, reproduce it, and fix it — from a report they never asked for. A good report gets triaged the same week. A bad one sits in a queue until it rots. This is the structure I use, and why each section exists.

Scope first. Everything here assumes written authorization for the target — a program policy you accepted, or an explicit agreement with the vendor. A report about a target you were never allowed to test is not a disclosure, it is a confession.

The shape of a report a vendor will read

Triage engineers skim. Give them four sections, in this order, and nothing they have to decode:

  1. Summary — one paragraph: what the issue is, in plain words.
  2. Impact — what an attacker could concretely do with it. Facts, not movie plots.
  3. Reproduction steps — the exact sequence that produces the behavior, copy-pasteable.
  4. Remediation suggestion — a concrete fix, or at least the direction of one.

Evidence (request/response pairs, screenshots, timestamps) is attached or referenced inline. Keep the report under two pages unless the chain genuinely needs more.

Reproduction steps: write for a stranger

The person triaging your report does not have your setup, your cookies, or your memory. Every step must be executable by someone who has never seen the target. I write steps as literal commands with the real requests I sent:

$ curl -s -i "https://target.example.com/api/v1/session/check" \
  -H "Cookie: session=MY_OWN_TEST_SESSION" \
  -D check.headers -o check.body

$ cat check.headers
HTTP/1.1 200 OK
content-type: application/json
...

$ python3 -m json.tool check.body

Rules I hold myself to:

  • Only my own accounts and my own data. No other user's records, no real customer data, no harvested credentials — redacted or otherwise.
  • State every prerequisite. "Logged in as my test account" means nothing if the reader does not know how that account was created. Say how.
  • Show the response, not just the request. A 200 OK with an empty body is a different story than a 200 with data. Paste both sides.
  • One variable changes at a time. If the report is about what changes between authenticated and unauthenticated, show both requests and both responses side by side.

Evidence is dated notes, not memories

"I remember seeing this on Tuesday" is not evidence. A dated note with the exact command, the timestamp, and the raw response is. During an engagement I keep everything in one folder, in chronological order:

$ ls 2026-10-16/
01-prelogin-request.txt
02-prelogin-response.txt
03-session-check-request.txt
04-session-check-response.txt
notes.md

When I write the report, the steps are already there — I am transcribing, not reconstructing. Screenshots get the same treatment: full context visible (URL bar, timestamp), sensitive values redacted before the report leaves my machine, originals kept locally.

Impact: state facts, let them score it

The most common failure in reports is severity inflation. "Critical: complete database compromise" for a verbose error message makes the triager distrust the whole report. Describe what you actually demonstrated, with the exact data you touched:

  • Good: "An unauthenticated request to this endpoint returns the email address of the account passed in the parameter. I confirmed this with two test accounts I created myself."
  • Bad: "This could potentially allow attackers to compromise all users."

If the program uses CVSS, suggest a vector and say it is a suggestion. Never argue severity in the report — a calm, factual impact section does the arguing for you.

Tone: professional, patient, disposable

A disclosure report is a cold email to someone who is already busy. No hype, no demands, no countdown ultimatums. State what you found, how to fix it, and that you are available for follow-up questions. If the vendor does not respond, follow the program's policy on timelines — and keep a dated record of every message you sent.

One more rule I never break: do not publish until it is fixed or you have explicit permission. The write-ups on this blog describe methodology and design thinking. Live findings go to the vendor first, privately, and only appear here after remediation or permission — or never.
