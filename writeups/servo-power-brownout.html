Servos need their own power supply

The symptom looks like a software bug. The sketch uploads fine, the ESP32 boots, the serial output flows — and the moment a servo starts moving, the board resets. Sometimes it resets once and recovers. Sometimes it resets in a loop, twitching the servo a few degrees on every boot. What changed is the load on the power rail: a servo is a motor, and motors do not sip current politely.

Scope first. Everything here assumes written authorization for the target. No scope, no recon.

Why the shared rail fails

A small hobby servo such as an SG90 draws little current at rest, a few hundred milliamps while moving freely, and roughly 650 mA at stall — when it starts moving, changes direction, or pushes against a load. An MG90S is in the same ballpark, and the numbers add up fast with two or three servos on one robot head or arm. The 5V pin of a typical ESP32 development board cannot deliver that: it is fed, directly or through an on-board regulator, from a USB port usually limited to about 500 mA.

So the servo starts, the current demand spikes, the rail voltage sags, and the ESP32's brownout detector trips. The chip resets, the servo loses its signal, the rail recovers, the board boots again — and the cycle repeats. The subtler symptom is no reset at all, but a servo that jitters, hums, or stops short of its commanded angle, because the voltage is just low enough to starve the motor without tripping the detector. Either way, do not debug the sketch first. It is almost certainly fine.

Recognize it before you rewire anything

The fastest diagnostic is the serial monitor. A brownout reset announces itself in the boot log, and the pattern — normal output, then a sudden reset exactly when the servo moves — is the signature:

$ arduino-cli monitor -p /dev/ttyUSB0 -c baudrate=115200
Moving servo to 90 degrees...
Brownout detector was triggered
rst:0x10 (RTCWDT_RTC_RESET),boot:0x13 (SPI_FAST_FLASH_BOOT)
ets Jul 29 2019 12:21:46

Two more checks confirm it without any instruments. First, disconnect the servo's power wire and run the sketch again: if the board stops resetting, the servo load was the cause, not the code. Second, move the servo by hand (power off) and feel for resistance — a jammed horn or a servo fighting its end-stop draws stall current continuously. If you have a multimeter, watch the 5V rail while the servo moves: a dip of a volt or more under load is all the proof you need.

The fix: a separate supply, one common ground

Power the servos from their own 5V supply — a 5V 2A wall adapter, a UBEC, or a battery pack rated for the load — and power the ESP32 from USB as usual. The servo's red wire goes to the servo supply's positive terminal, the brown or black wire to its negative terminal, and only the orange or yellow signal wire goes to the ESP32 GPIO. Then join the two grounds: a wire from the servo supply's negative terminal to an ESP32 GND pin. That last wire is not optional. Without a common ground, the signal wire has no reference voltage, and the servo will ignore commands or jitter randomly even though both supplies are healthy.

A large electrolytic capacitor across the servo supply rails — 470 µF to 1000 µF, mind the polarity — helps absorb brief current spikes when a servo starts moving. It is a supplement, not a substitute: it cannot rescue an undersized supply, but it smooths the edges on a correctly sized one. Keep the servo power leads short and reasonably thick, too; thin jumper wires add resistance exactly where a spike hurts most.

Size the supply for stall, not for idle

Budget with stall currents, because servos hit them every time they start, reverse, or stall against a load. A rough rule: one SG90 or MG90S needs about 1 A of headroom, so a single servo wants a 5V 1A supply at minimum, two servos want 2A, and a four-servo arm wants 3A or more. If the supply is only rated for the running current, the first simultaneous move of two servos will drag the rail down again and the servos will stutter. When in doubt, go one size up.

Verify it the same way it failed

After rewiring, run the exact motion that used to reset the board — a full sweep from one end-stop to the other, ideally with all servos moving together — and watch the serial monitor:

#include <ESP32Servo.h>

Servo myServo;

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  myServo.attach(13);
}

void loop() {
  myServo.write(35);
  delay(1000);
  myServo.write(145);
  delay(1000);
}

If the board stays up through repeated sweeps, the serial output keeps flowing, and the servo reaches both ends of its travel without jitter, the fix held. If it still resets, check the two mistakes that survive this fix most often: the grounds are not actually joined, or the servo supply itself is undersized. Servos are motors. Feed them like motors, and the microcontroller gets to do its own job in peace.
